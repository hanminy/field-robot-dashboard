Advancements and Challenges in Earthmoving Equipment Automation Using Core Technologies

Authors: Jiyu Feng, Wei Chen, Changyi Liu, Xinhui Liu, Bingwei Cao, Feixiang Xu

Journal: Automation in Construction 179 (2025) 106472

Affiliations: - School of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering / National Key Laboratory of Automotive Chassis Integration and Bionics, Jilin University, Changchun 130025, China - Key Laboratory of Bionic Engineering, Ministry of Education, Jilin University, Changchun 130025, China - School of Mechanical and Vehicle Engineering, Changchun University, Changchun 130025, China - School of Information and Control Engineering, China University of Mining and Technology, Xuzhou 221116, China

Abstract

Earthmoving equipment automation is limited by interactions with complex media, unstructured terrain and highly variable environmental conditions. Although research in this field has continued for nearly thirty years, a truly fully autonomous system remains unrealized. This paper examines automation technologies for earthmoving equipment in typical repetitive short-cycle loading operations and presents a systematic survey of core literature spanning over thirty years. Key techniques are grouped into four categories: environmental perception, path planning and tracking, autonomous loading and unloading, and safety and risk assessment. The analysis indicates that current research primarily addresses improvements to individual stages or subsystems while system-level optimization of the entire workflow is still lacking. Moreover, data-physics hybrid methods show strong potential in earthmoving equipment automation applications by compensating for errors in physical models and improving the generalization of data-driven models. Finally, the major challenges are summarized and directions for future research are proposed.

Keywords: Earthmoving equipment, Automation, Short-cycle loading, Sensing technology

1. Introduction

Earthmoving equipment is widely used in various environments, including mining operations, construction sites, railway freight yards, and agricultural estates, primarily for excavation and loading tasks. In earthworks projects, wheel loaders and excavators typically work in conjunction with dump trucks to perform short-cycle loading tasks for material transport. These tasks are inherently monotonous and repetitive, and prolonged driving operations can lead to driver distraction and physical fatigue, affecting reaction time and judgment, potentially decreasing productivity and causing safety incidents. Additionally, the dust, noise, and vibrations generated during earthmoving operations pose significant occupational health and safety risks to operators.

As a result, the automation of earthmoving equipment has become an area of significant research interest, particularly with the continuous advancements in autonomous driving technology for passenger vehicles. However, despite extensive research, commercial earthmoving machinery automation systems suitable for practical application have yet to materialize, constrained by factors such as environmental complexity, perception capabilities, autonomous decision-making and operational strategies, and safety.

2. Problem Definition and Decomposition

Unlike autonomous vehicles, which confront complex traffic environments, automated earthmoving equipment typically operates within enclosed and strictly controlled work sites. The driving environment is relatively simplified, thereby facilitating the implementation of automation technologies. However, the functional characteristics of earthmoving equipment dictate that, in addition to autonomous driving capabilities, they must also perform material loading and short-distance transportation — a fundamental deviation from the core tasks of autonomous vehicles.

2.1 Wheel Loader Short-Cycle Loading Workflow

The short-cycle loading operation of a wheel loader can be subdivided into six principal phases:

  1. Approach and site assessment: Ascertain the location and geometric configuration of the material stockpile and the parking position of the dump truck; select the most appropriate excavation point and plan the optimal access route.

  2. Excavation and loading: Select an appropriate digging trajectory based on material type and size to ensure the front bucket is fully loaded. Adjust the operating strategy in real-time to prevent tire slippage or insufficient loading.

  3. Retreat and route planning: Assess the relative position of the loader and the dump truck; plan the optimal route for retreat from the stockpile to the dump truck. This is critical for reducing cycle time, lowering fuel consumption, and enhancing operational efficiency.

  4. Approach and pre-assessment of dump truck: Pre-assess the distribution of material on the truck to ensure an even spread during unloading and prevent spillage. Rapidly raise the boom so that the bucket's elevation surpasses the truck body, while maintaining appropriate clearance.

  5. Unloading and material adjustment: Tilt the material within the bucket as needed and make any necessary adjustments to the material on the dump truck to meet subsequent transportation requirements.

  6. Return and preparation: Maneuver the loader in reverse to move away from the dump truck and lower the bucket in preparation for the subsequent short-cycle loading operation.

As achieving a fully loaded dump truck typically necessitates multiple short-cycle loading operations, the geometry of the material stockpile, the selection of excavation points, and the choice of travel paths exhibit dynamic changes as the number of scooping operations increases. This scenario necessitates that the wheel loader be equipped with an array of high-precision sensors, which, in combination with data fusion techniques, enable real-time comprehensive assessments to underpin accurate autonomous decision-making.

3. Research Methodology

This paper employs a hybrid methodology that combines bibliometric analysis and systematic review. The paper systematically searches the relevant literature from academic databases and uses keyword co-occurrence and cluster analysis for bibliometric analysis to identify key research areas and trends. Representative literature is then systematically reviewed to gain a comprehensive understanding of the evolution of the field.

4. Core Technologies for Autonomous Operations

Following the six phases of the wheel loader's short-cycle loading operation, the key technologies are organized into four parts: environmental perception (4.1), path planning and tracking (4.2), autonomous loading and unloading (4.3), and safety and risk assessment (4.4). Although these technologies are discussed separately, they are not isolated; they are closely interconnected and mutually supportive.

4.1 Environmental Perception

Environmental perception primarily involves the use of sensors such as cameras, LiDAR, and millimeter-wave radar to perceive unstructured roads, identify and reconstruct material piles, assess excavation difficulty, and recognize vehicle pose. This capability forms the foundation for the autonomous operation of earthmoving equipment.

4.1.1 Perception of Unstructured Roads

Earthmoving equipment mainly operates in unstructured environments such as construction sites and mines, where it is often impossible to perform road recognition using uniform typical features as on urban roads or highways. Factors such as unclear road boundaries, irregular shapes, deteriorated pavement appearance, variable lighting, and changing backgrounds pose great challenges.

Three categories of research:

Category Methods Advantages Limitations
Computer vision-based Model-based, feature-based, machine learning-based (e.g., RD-Net semantic segmentation, attention modules) Rich information content, low cost Susceptible to lighting/weather changes; large image databases needed
LiDAR-based Semantic segmentation with attention mechanism (Liu et al.) Not affected by lighting conditions; rich point cloud data Limited research; ideal accuracy not yet achieved
Multimodal fusion Low-level and high-level feature fusion from images and point clouds (Ye et al.) Effective complementarity; improved accuracy and real-time responsiveness Higher system complexity

Research on road perception for earthmoving equipment in unstructured environments is currently scarce but is a key component for enabling autonomous operations.

4.1.2 Pile Identification and Contour Reconstruction

Rapid and accurate identification of stockpiles and reconstruction of their contours are of great significance for determining the optimal excavation point, planning the operational path, evaluating operational efficiency, and achieving fully autonomous operation.

  • Proprioceptive sensors: Using IMUs to measure acceleration data for material classification (Artan et al.); pressure sensors to distinguish between material types (Fernando et al.); energy and motion data from excavators to classify four different materials (Liu et al.); limited to single-material classification

4.1.3 Excavation Difficulty Assessment

Excavation difficulty assessment enables equipment and cooperating dump trucks to make more accurate autonomous decisions. This area is closely related to material identification and pile reconstruction.

4.1.4 Vehicle Pose Recognition

Accurate pose recognition of cooperating vehicles (especially dump trucks) is essential for planning unloading maneuvers and maintaining appropriate clearances.

4.2 Path Planning and Tracking

4.2.1 Path Planning

Path planning addresses route generation for Phase I (approaching the material pile), Phase III (moving from the pile to the dump truck), and Phase VI (returning from the dump truck to the staging point). It involves both point-to-point (PTP) planning and complete coverage path planning (CCPP).

Global path planning: Uses known positions of the pile, dump truck, and equipment to create a collision-free base route optimized for shortest distance and efficiency.

Local path planning: Adjusts the route in real-time based on the machine's status and surrounding conditions.

Key algorithms:

Algorithm Characteristics
Dijkstra Deterministic and consistent; widely used for global planning
A* Heuristic search; efficient for grid maps
RRT / Goal-directed RRT (improved) Sampling-based; suitable for high-dimensional spaces with complex constraints
Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) Population-based optimization
Ant Colony Algorithm (improved) Bio-inspired; suitable for combinatorial problems
Reinforcement Learning Adaptive; learns from environment interaction; for path + energy optimization
MPC (Model Predictive Control) Online; considers constraints and disturbances

Industry examples: - Kajima A4CSEL: robust tracking control with heading estimation; field trials with autonomous rollers and bulldozers achieved path-tracking accuracy within +/-10 cm - Sakai Heavy Industries + JIG-SAW: autonomous roller with path-tracking deviations ~20 cm; compaction coverage ~3.5x higher than manual operation

4.2.2 Path Tracking

Path tracking ensures the vehicle follows the planned trajectory accurately and smoothly. Methods include: - Feedback linearized iterative learning (Dekker et al.) - LQR controller - PSO-NMPC control strategy - Pure tracking + fuzzy control (for unmanned electric shovels) - Nonlinear Model Predictive Control

4.3 Autonomous Loading and Unloading

4.3.1 Selection of Excavation Points

Excavation point selection involves identifying the optimal digging locations and orientations on a material pile based on its geometric shape. Reliable excavation points significantly impact the fill factor, safety, energy consumption, vehicle structural damage, travel distance, and number of excavation cycles.

Principles for reducing candidate excavation points: 1. Select points on the pile boundary 2. Choose locations adjacent to the previous excavation point to shorten travel distance 3. Choose the candidate point that minimizes the drop edge length of the pile after excavation 4. The direction of the excavation point should be perpendicular to the slope of the pile 5. Choose candidate points that exhibit a certain degree of convexity

Main methods:

Method Description Reference
Column model + bucket trajectory Computes moment acting on bucket to evaluate each potential excavation posture; minimizes total moment Sarata et al.
Two-section bucket method Divides bucket into left and right; evaluates convexity and inclination Singh et al.
Three-section bucket method Subdivides into left, middle, right; more accurate description of convex/concave regions; also proposes quadratic fitting model Magnusson et al.
GA-based optimization with slope/distance metrics Incorporates slope and travel distance evaluation metrics Chen et al.
3D configuration score space Maps path length values to score space to identify optimal excavation point Takei et al.
Centroid evaluation Combines pile edge curves with height field; uses material centroid position within bucket Lindmark et al.
Height-field grid method Considers multiple metrics: distance, collision, spatial area, fill volume Cardenas et al.
Reinforcement Learning Reward function based on convexity; typically integrated with path planning Backman et al., Kawabe et al.

Trajectory planning involves generating a time-parameterized motion profile along a predefined path. Studies show the excavation phase accounts for approximately one-third of total energy consumption in the entire operation cycle. Experienced operators can achieve fuel efficiency improvements of up to 150% and productivity increases of up to 300%.

5. Major Challenges and Future Directions

5.1 Key Challenges in Practice

5.1.1 Limitations of Heterogeneous Sensor Data Fusion

To achieve comprehensive and accurate site perception, autonomous earthmoving equipment combines data from multiple sensors (stereo vision cameras, LiDAR, GPS, and more). These sources are highly complementary, but each uses different formats and stores data separately, making fusion the key to high-precision environment sensing. Key challenges during fusion: - Mismatched resolutions - Alignment errors - Uneven sensor quality - Transmission delays

Additionally, autonomy demands real-time performance, requiring raw sensor streams to be dimension-reduced, stripped of redundancy, and distilled into key features before fusion.

Future direction: A layered fusion architecture built on edge computing and cloud collaboration: - On-site edge nodes align sensor data in time and space, normalize resolutions, and perform initial dimension reduction - Only the most valuable compressed features are sent to the cloud - Hybrid fusion algorithm (blending data-driven and knowledge-driven methods) merges inputs into high-level representations

5.1.2 Insufficient Material Identification of Stockpiles and Diversified Excavation Strategies

Stockpile characteristics depend not only on inherent physical properties but also on external factors such as weather conditions (dry/loose soil vs. wet/muddy soil). Raw materials also vary widely in size, and interactions between stones of different dimensions and the bucket are complex.

Future direction: Combined digital twin and human-machine collaboration: - Digital twin simulates material properties and bucket contact mechanics, generating diverse large-scale synthetic data for training - In the field, when the system detects unfamiliar or extreme materials, a skilled operator demonstrates or fine-tunes the digging motion - Demonstrations are uploaded in real-time to edge nodes and synced to the cloud to update models and strategies

5.1.3 Lack of a Universal Safety and Risk Assessment Framework

Existing safety and risk assessment tools typically focus on personnel monitoring or static environmental sensing, but do not provide a comprehensive evaluation of major hazards such as underground utilities, landslides, or tunnel collapses.

Required framework with three core modules: 1. Personnel monitoring 2. Environmental sensing 3. Construction risk evaluation - With extensions tailored to mining, tunnel excavation, and high-slope operations - Standardized interface standards and data protocols enabling plug-and-play components - Swappable submodules (landslide prediction, collapse detection) that can be interchanged to match project needs - Validate module performance within a digital twin virtual platform

5.1.4 Lack of High-Quality, Large-Scale Datasets for Earthmoving Equipment

Existing collections fail to capture the full range of construction scenarios (various equipment types, pile geometries, unstructured terrain), limiting deep-learning models' generalization and robustness.

Future direction: - Digital twin platforms simulate diverse job-site conditions and produce precisely labeled synthetic data for cameras, LiDAR, and IMUs - Generative AI models apply domain randomization and detail augmentation to create training data resembling real-world conditions - Edge nodes collect key real-world snippets; mixed training on real and synthetic data substantially improves model stability and robustness

5.2 Future Development Directions

5.2.1 Digital Twin

Digital twin technology creates virtual models of autonomous earthmoving equipment, enabling real-time mapping of machine status and environmental changes to support operation simulation, strategy optimization, and preventive maintenance.

Industry example: DeepX Corporation (Japan) achieved automated underground excavation by combining digital twin visualization with autonomous excavators. Their system collects site data via LiDAR and GNSS, uses point-cloud recognition to visualize terrain, equipment position, and motion in 3D, synchronizing the physical world with the digital twin in real-time. The platform also computes key performance metrics and generates reports for remote monitoring.

Future focus: Enhancing model accuracy and live-update capability to enable more precise simulations and intelligent decision-making.

5.2.2 Edge Computing and Cloud Collaboration

Deploying edge computing devices on construction sites enables preprocessing, dimensionality reduction, and fusion of massive data from LiDAR, cameras, IMUs, and GPS directly on site. This delivers real-time feedback and mitigates the effects of network latency and instability. Cloud platforms use superior computing and storage resources to perform deep analysis on long-term accumulated data, refining algorithms and continuously updating models.

Industry example: Kajima's A4CSEL system (October 2021) — four operators in the centralized control center at Kajima's Tokyo headquarters managed twenty autonomous machines across three sites (Akita, Nara, and Kanagawa prefectures) around the clock, achieving notable gains in efficiency, safety, and consistency.

Supported by 5G and 6G networks, communication efficiency and reliability will further improve.

5.2.3 Standardization and Modular Design

The lack of a unified communication standard hampers efficient collaboration between machines from multiple manufacturers using different sensors, control systems, and data protocols. Required elements: - Standardized data-communication protocols: IIoT protocols, CAN bus, OPC UA - Open-source construction technology platforms

Industry example: Japan Civil Engineering Research Institute's OPERA (Open Platform for Earthwork with Robotics and Autonomy) integrates standardized control signals, hardware retrofit kits, wireless communication modules, simulators, and middleware; has successfully demonstrated autonomous excavator loading operations.

Roadmap for standardization: 1. Establish unified domestic standards for OEMs and industry applications 2. Work with international bodies (ISO, IEC) to develop compatible, interoperable global protocols 3. Build shared open-source code repositories, simulation tools, and standardized datasets 4. Form multinational, interdisciplinary alliances among research institutions, equipment manufacturers, and construction firms 5. Promote certification and mutual recognition schemes for equipment, systems, and operators

5.2.4 Human-Machine Collaboration

Future autonomous earthmoving equipment will treat human operators and intelligent systems as complementary partners rather than mere replacements. In relatively uniform, high-repetition environments (quarries, concrete batching plants), automation performs effectively; however, in complex and dynamic construction settings, operators remain essential for strategic planning, anomaly handling, and emergency decision-making.

Industry example: Kajima's A4CSEL (July 2024) fully automated every task at a mountain tunnel face: an autonomous wheel loader scoops blasted material and transfers it to a rear dump truck; when load falls below a set threshold, operators in the remote-control room deploy a teleoperated excavator to gather scattered debris before resuming autonomous scooping. This collaborative approach boosts efficiency and safety while providing flexibility for continual refinement.

Emerging commercial systems: - Built Robotics Inc.: fully autonomous pile-driving robot RPD 35 operates at 5x the speed of a human crew - SafeAI Inc.: conversion of the Caterpillar 725 electric articulated dump truck delivers 13% improvement in on-site environmental performance - ARAV Inc.: excavator automation kits (sales planned from June 2025); retrofits excavators and loaders from major OEMs (Caterpillar, John Deere, Hitachi)

6. Conclusions

This paper provided a comprehensive review of the current state of research on automation technologies for earthmoving equipment, with a particular focus on advances in environmental perception, path planning and tracking, autonomous loading and unloading, and safety and risk assessment.

Key Findings

  1. Sensor fusion is fundamental: To operate autonomously in complex and uncertain environments, earthmoving equipment must rely on multisource information acquisition systems (computer vision, LiDAR, real-time dynamic positioning, pressure sensors) and utilize data fusion techniques to acquire comprehensive environmental information. Challenges including mismatches in sensor resolution, data alignment errors, inconsistent data quality, and transmission delays significantly hinder effective data fusion.

  2. Individual subsystem optimization dominates: Current research primarily addresses improvements to individual stages or subsystems; system-level optimization of the entire workflow is still lacking. Researchers urgently need to undertake a holistic optimization of the entire workflow.

  3. Data-physics hybrid methods show strong potential: These methods compensate for errors in physical models and improve the generalization of data-driven models.

  4. Key challenges identified: - Limitations in the fusion of data from heterogeneous sensors - Insufficient sophistication in the recognition of stockpile material composition and deployment of diverse digging strategies - Absence of a universal framework for safety and risk assessment - Lack of high-quality, large-scale datasets

  5. Digging strategies are critical: Adopting appropriate digging strategies tailored to the characteristics of the stockpiled material is critical for enhancing operational efficiency; these strategies are intrinsically linked to physical material properties and significantly influenced by external factors such as weather conditions.

  6. Safety requires more attention: Safety, as the fundamental prerequisite for earthmoving automation, has hitherto received insufficient attention; the establishment of a universal framework for safety and risk assessment is of considerable practical significance.

State of Commercial Deployment

Significant theoretical advances have been made, but many breakthroughs remain confined to simulations or laboratory tests: - Volvo prototype: automated cooperation between an articulated tractor and a wheel loader achieved just 70% of a skilled operator's digging and unloading efficiency - ETH Zurich hydraulic excavator: autonomously built a standalone stone wall (10 m x 1.7 m x 4 m) and a permanent retaining wall (65.5 m x 1.8 m x 6 m) - University of Tokyo + Seikei University: earth-transport experiment with multiple autonomous machines; when a team's performance dropped, the lead excavator re-allocated resources by coordinating with other teams, maintaining overall productivity in real-time - Kajima A4CSEL: achieved full-process automation (bulldozers, rollers, dump trucks) at Naruse Dam and the Kamigo test tunnel, allowing a single operator to manage multiple machines

Future Outlook

  1. Digital twin technology will create virtual models enabling real-time mapping of machine status and environmental changes
  2. Edge-and-cloud collaborative architecture will balance on-site real-time performance with big-data analytics advantages; supported by 5G and 6G networks
  3. Standardized, modular designs will ease retrofitting of legacy machines and enable seamless interoperability
  4. Human-machine collaboration is likely to become the dominant mode in complex construction scenarios — autonomy does not replace operators but frees them from tedious, dangerous tasks to focus on higher-level decision-making and management
